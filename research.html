Chromatin and neuron identity

Every cell in an animal carries the same genome, yet a neuron uses a very different set of genes from a muscle or gut cell. Much of that choice is made through chromatin, the way DNA is packaged and marked. In the sea anemone Nematostella vectensis, I study how chromatin regulators switch neural genes on and off as cells become neurons.

Approaches: ChIP-seq, bulk and single-cell RNA-seq, transgenic lines, confocal imaging.

Neural regeneration

Cut a Nematostella in half and each piece regrows the missing body, including its nervous system. This makes it a powerful system for asking how neurons are rebuilt in an adult animal. Neural regeneration is a growing part of my research. I compare two ways the adult animal makes neurons: regeneration after injury, and adult neurogenesis, the continuous production of new neurons throughout life. I want to know which programmes the two share, what is different between them, and what role chromatin plays in switching them on.

How neurons are specified

A progenitor cell takes a series of genetic steps to become a particular type of neuron. During my PhD I followed these steps for monoaminergic neurons, the cells that make dopamine and serotonin, in the developing Drosophila embryo.

Approaches: single-cell RNA-seq, imaging, genetics.

Thesis: How to build a nervous system? (University of Leicester, 2024)

Papers: Lewis et al. 2025, bioRxiv · Goulty, Lewis et al. 2025, bioRxiv

Evolution of complexity

Comparing animals that split hundreds of millions of years ago shows which parts of neural development are shared and which are new inventions. I use phylogenomics and comparative transcriptomics across sponges, cnidarians and insects to ask how nervous systems arose.

Approaches: phylogenomics, comparative transcriptomics.

Papers: Aleotti et al. 2024, Life Science Alliance · Zadra et al. 2023, Insects
